The 8.41 that wasn't.

8.41
The test I first ran
0.87
The corrected test

I built a program that watches trading in S&P 500 options and guesses whether the market will rise or fall over the next 15 minutes. Statisticians usually treat a score above 2 as a sign that an effect is real. My program scored 8.41. Once I stopped counting the same price moves over and over, it scored 0.87. That is noise.

The mistake

My program checked the market every 15 seconds, and I graded each check on the next 15 minutes of price. Checks 15 seconds apart are graded on almost the same 15 minutes.

So the test thought it had far more evidence than it did. My 82,183 graded checks carried about as much information as 2,986 independent ones.

It is like asking one friend the same question over and over and calling it a survey.

How I caught it

The clue was in the pattern. A real signal should not get more certain just because each check looks further ahead.

It was a Saturday, a few days after my first full backtest finished. The 15-minute horizon came back at t = 8.41. For about ten minutes I thought I had found something real. Then I looked at the size of the effect. It was 0.43 basis points, roughly one tick in S&P futures. An effect that small should not come with that much certainty.

The second thing I noticed was the pattern across horizons. At 5 minutes the t-stat was 4.29. At 15 minutes it jumped to 8.41, and at 30 it hit 8.61. The sample size barely changed between them. The only thing that changed was how far forward each window reached. Longer windows were making the result look stronger, and that made no sense for a real signal.

That pointed me at the windows themselves. I had about 550 polls a day, and each one was scored on the next 15 minutes of price. So every 15-minute move in the market was being counted around 20 times. My 82,183 observations were really the same few thousand moves, repeated.

The first thing I checked was the simplest fix I could think of. I averaged each day into a single number and reran the test on 150 days instead of 82,183 polls. The t-stat fell to 0.87. Only 53% of days were positive. What looked like a discovery was a coin flip I had counted twenty times.

What it means

The signal shows no detectable predictive power. The study had an 80% chance of detecting an average effect of 0.64 basis points, so a real effect smaller than that cannot be ruled out.

The trap is not about trading. Anything graded on overlapping windows can fool a standard test the same way, such as a 7-day average that gets recomputed every day. Economists worked out the correction for overlapping forecasts in 1980 (Hansen and Hodrick). I ran into the problem on my own data, then measured how fast you can sample before the standard test breaks.

What I still want to know

  1. The amount of false confidence can be predicted from how correlated neighbouring checks are. That prediction tracks the measured rate but runs a few points low. Why?
  2. Is there a real effect smaller than 0.64 basis points that a longer sample could find?

Counts. 211,739 polls in 152 logged sessions. Most polls were flat; the test uses the 82,183 long or short polls with a valid 15-minute return, over 150 trading days. Their autocorrelation implies about 2,986 independent observations.

Why day clustering. The signal tends to stay long or short for long stretches within a day, longer than the 15-minute lag window Newey-West uses here. Treating each day as one observation allows any dependence within a day and none across days. A day bootstrap gives a 95% interval of −0.24 to +0.64 basis points.

Two placebo figures. The paper reports 77.5% of placebo signals passing the naive test, from 200 draws. The slider uses 1,000 draws with a different seed and gets 74.3%. Both use the same within-day circular shift of the real signal.

Two no-overlap figures. The paper's 0.34 keeps a poll only if it is at least 15 minutes after the last kept poll. The slider's 15-minute point keeps the first poll in each 15-minute clock interval, which gives a different subsample (t = 0.88). Both are far below 1.96.

Reproducibility. One script recomputes the day-level results from committed data on every change. A full rebuild from the 152 raw session logs reproduced every statistic in the paper.
