The 8.41 that wasn't

Dhruv Goyani · American Heritage School, Class of 2027 · Presented at the 2026 IEEE 20th International Conference on Computer Science and Information Technologies (CSIT), October 2026

I built a program that reads S&P 500 option orders and says long, short, or flat. Over 150 trading days it looked like it could predict the next 15 minutes. The standard test gave t = 8.41. Anything above 1.96 usually counts as significant.

It was not real. My program checked the market every 15 seconds, so each 15-minute window overlapped about 67 others. The test counted the same price move dozens of times and thought it had far more evidence than it did.

A test that works should fool you about 5 times in 100. The fair test, which treats each trading day as one piece of evidence, stays near in 100 at every speed.

Same signal, same days. The only thing that changes is how often I look. Checking faster made the standard test more confident and more wrong. The fair test never comes close to 1.96.

The paper's result

Test at the 15-minute horizont
Standard, every check treated as independent8.41
Newey-West (corrects for overlap)1.79
Day-clustered (150 trading days)0.87
Only returns that share no window (2,487)0.34

The answer is a null. The signal shows no detectable predictive power, and the study had 80% power to detect a mean signed return of 0.64 basis points.

This is an old problem. Lars Peter Hansen and Robert Hodrick worked out how to correct for overlapping forecast windows in 1980. I ran into it on my own data, then measured how fast you can sample before the standard test breaks.
